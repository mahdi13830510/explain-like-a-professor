    Navigation · A complete lesson

    How GPS Knows Where You Are

    Your phone has no compass pointing at its own coordinates and no map pin built in. All it can do is listen to faint radio voices from space, each saying only “I am here, and it is exactly this time.” From nothing more than that, it works out where it is to within a few metres.

    The path: what a satellite actually broadcasts → turning a travel time into a distance → one distance is a circle, three pin you down → why the phone's cheap clock ruins this → the fix: treat the clock error as one more unknown, and solve by iteration → why the arrangement of satellites matters → why Einstein had to be built into the satellites → what still goes wrong in a city.
    Assumed: distance = speed × time, Pythagoras, and the idea of solving equations for unknowns. The math stays light, but nothing is hand-waved.

    1The problem: a phone that knows nothing

    Switch on a phone in the middle of a field. It has no idea whether it is in Lisbon or Lagos. It cannot see landmarks, it has no odometer, and nothing inside it measures position directly. Yet within seconds a blue dot appears on the map, usually within a few metres of where you are actually standing. Where does that information come from?

    Not from the satellites telling the phone where it is. The satellites have no idea the phone exists; they never receive anything from it. GPS is strictly one-way: the satellites broadcast, and any number of receivers listen. That is why a billion phones can use it at once without anything being overloaded, and also why the phone has to do all the work of turning those broadcasts into a position.

    So the real question of this lesson is: if all you can do is listen, what could you possibly hear that tells you where you are? Every step below is the answer to some version of that question, and each one exposes the next problem to solve.

    2What a satellite actually says

    The Global Positioning System is a constellation run by the United States, designed around at least 24 operational satellites (in recent years there have usually been about 31). They orbit about 20,200 km above the ground, which is high enough that each one circles the Earth in just under 12 hours, moving at roughly 3.9 km/s. The orbits are arranged so that, from almost anywhere on Earth at any time, at least four satellites are above the horizon.

    Each satellite continuously transmits a radio signal that carries, in effect, two pieces of information:

    “I am here.” The satellite broadcasts the parameters of its own orbit (called the ephemeris), precise enough that a receiver can calculate where the satellite was at any instant. Ground stations track every satellite and upload fresh orbit data regularly, so this is trustworthy.

    “It is exactly this time.” Each satellite carries atomic clocks, and the signal is built so that a receiver can tell precisely when each piece of it left the satellite. Every satellite's clock is monitored from the ground, and small corrections to it are broadcast too, so all the satellites effectively share one very accurate time scale, GPS time.

    That's all. No satellite says “you are 21,000 km away from me” and none says anything about the receiver. The position must be deduced. The signal is also astonishingly faint by the time it reaches the ground, weaker than the background radio noise around it. A receiver finds it anyway because each satellite repeats a known pseudo-random code: the receiver slides its own copy of the code along in time until it lines up with what it hears, and the amount of sliding needed is what tells it when the signal arrived.

    Key idea

    A satellite gives you two facts: where it was, and when it sent the signal. You add a third: when the signal arrived. Everything GPS does is built from those three.

    3Time of flight becomes distance

    If you know when a signal left and when it arrived, you know how long it travelled. Radio waves travel at the speed of light, and the speed of light is not just measured to high accuracy; since 1983 it has been fixed by the definition of the metre at exactly

    $$c = 299{,}792{,}458\ \text{m/s}.$$

    So the most familiar formula in physics, distance = speed × time, turns a travel time $\Delta t$ into a distance:

    $$d = c\,\Delta t.$$

    Put in some numbers to get a feel for the scale. A satellite directly overhead is about 20,200 km away, so its signal takes $20{,}200{,}000 / 299{,}792{,}458 \approx 0.0674$ s, about 67 milliseconds, to reach you. A satellite just on the horizon is further away, about 25,800 km, so its signal takes about 86 ms. Every GPS signal you ever receive has been in flight for somewhere in that range.

    Now turn it around and ask the question that will haunt the rest of this lesson: how much distance does a tiny timing error cost? Light covers $299{,}792{,}458 \times 10^{-9} \approx 0.30$ m in one nanosecond. So:

    timing errordistance error $= c \times$ error
    1 nanosecond (10⁻⁹ s)≈ 30 cm
    10 nanoseconds≈ 3 m
    1 microsecond (10⁻⁶ s)≈ 300 m
    1 millisecond (10⁻³ s)≈ 300 km

    Notice how brutal that conversion rate is. To know a distance to within a few metres, you need to know the travel time to within about ten billionths of a second. Keep that number in mind; it is the root of the two biggest problems later on.

    Careful · what “knowing the time” means

    The satellite stamps the departure time with its atomic clock. The arrival time can only be read from the clock inside the receiver. The formula $d = c\,\Delta t$ silently assumes those two clocks agree. Hold on to that assumption: section 5 is what happens when it is false, which, for a phone, it always is.

    4From distances to a position: trilateration

    Suppose for now that the clocks are perfect, so each satellite gives us a true distance. What does one distance tell us?

    It tells us we are somewhere on the set of points at that distance from the satellite. To keep pictures readable, this section works in a flat, two-dimensional toy world: three radio transmitters at known positions spread over a region about 60 km across. In two dimensions, “all points at distance $d$ from a transmitter” is a circle. (In three dimensions it is a sphere; we'll come back to that.) The geometry is the same as with satellites; only the scale changes, and the numbers in the figures are illustrative.

    One circle leaves infinitely many possibilities. Add a second transmitter and you must also be on its circle, so you are at a point where the two circles cross. Two circles generally cross at two points, mirror images across the line joining the transmitters. A third circle breaks the tie: only one of the two candidates is also at the right distance from the third transmitter. Finding a position from distances like this is called trilateration. (It's often mistakenly called triangulation, which means finding a position from measured angles; GPS never measures an angle.)

    How does a computer find the crossing point? Write the phone's unknown position as $p = (x, y)$ and transmitter $i$'s known position as $s_i = (x_i, y_i)$. Each measured distance $d_i$ gives one equation, straight from Pythagoras:

    $$d_i = \lvert p - s_i \rvert = \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2}, \qquad i = 1, 2, 3.$$

    Two unknowns, $x$ and $y$; three equations. The square roots make the equations nonlinear (that's why two circles can cross twice), so there's no single neat formula like solving $2x = 6$. We'll see how a receiver actually solves this kind of system in section 6, because the next problem changes the equations first.

    In three dimensions, each distance puts you on a sphere. Two spheres meet in a circle; a third sphere cuts that circle at two points. With satellites, one of the two points is usually absurd (far out in space, or moving at an impossible speed), so in principle three satellites and perfect clocks would be enough. That “in principle” is about to collapse.

    5The clock problem

    Each GPS satellite carries atomic clocks (rubidium or caesium), and the ground segment measures their tiny remaining errors and broadcasts corrections. A phone cannot carry an atomic clock. It keeps time with a quartz crystal oscillator, the same technology as a wristwatch, and quartz is good at counting seconds but nowhere near good enough to time light. Its exact rate depends on temperature, age and manufacturing, and an error of around one part in a million is a realistic order of magnitude (an illustrative figure; it varies between devices). One part in a million means the phone's clock gains or loses about 1 microsecond every second, and from the table above, one microsecond is 300 metres.

    Even setting the phone's clock from the mobile network doesn't come close: that is accurate to perhaps milliseconds, and a millisecond is 300 km. So the honest position is: the phone does not know the current time to anywhere near the accuracy GPS needs.

    Let's see exactly what that does to the measurements. Call the receiver's clock error $b$ (for bias): when true GPS time is $t$, the phone's clock reads $t + b$. The phone measures arrival on its own clock and subtracts the departure time stamped by the satellite:

    $$\Delta t_i^{\text{measured}} = \big(t^{\text{arrive}}_i + b\big) - t^{\text{depart}}_i$$
    arrival is read on the phone's clock, which is off by $b$; departure comes from the satellite's (corrected) atomic clock
    $$= \underbrace{\big(t^{\text{arrive}}_i - t^{\text{depart}}_i\big)}_{\text{true travel time}} + b$$
    regroup: the true flight time plus the clock error
    $$\rho_i = c\,\Delta t_i^{\text{measured}} = c\,(\text{true travel time}) + c\,b$$
    multiply by $c$ to turn the time into a distance, as in section 3
    $$\rho_i = \lvert p - s_i \rvert + c\,b$$
    $c \times$ true travel time is the true distance from the satellite at $s_i$ to the phone at $p$
    Definition · pseudorange

    The pseudorange to satellite $i$ is the distance the receiver computes from its own clock, $\rho_i = c \times (\text{arrival time on receiver clock} - \text{departure time})$. It equals the true range $\lvert p - s_i\rvert$ plus $c\,b$, where $p$ is the receiver's position, $s_i$ the satellite's position at departure, and $b$ the receiver's clock error in seconds. “Pseudo” because it is not the true distance, and the error is not random: it is the same $c\,b$ for every satellite, since all arrival times are read from the same faulty clock.

    That last point is the hinge of the whole lesson. If $b$ is 10 µs, then every pseudorange is about 3 km too long, all by the same amount. Draw circles with those radii and they no longer meet at the phone. They all swell together and overlap in a little curved triangle, and no single point is on all three.

    So a naive receiver is stuck. It has three circles that don't intersect and no idea which way to correct them. But notice what the widget shows: the circles aren't wrong in random ways. They're all wrong by one shared number. That's the clue.

    The bias cancels in differences

    Before fixing the problem, it's worth seeing a neat consequence of “same error for everyone”. Subtract two pseudoranges:

    $$\rho_1 - \rho_2 = \big(\lvert p - s_1\rvert + c\,b\big) - \big(\lvert p - s_2\rvert + c\,b\big)$$
    write out both pseudoranges
    $$= \lvert p - s_1\rvert - \lvert p - s_2\rvert$$
    the $c\,b$ terms are identical, so they cancel exactly, whatever $b$ is

    The difference of two pseudoranges is exactly the difference of the two true ranges. The bad clock has dropped out. Geometrically, “my distance to $s_1$ minus my distance to $s_2$ equals a known number” describes a hyperbola (a hyperboloid in 3-D), not a circle. With $n$ satellites you get $n-1$ independent differences, and each pins you to a hyperbola. In 2-D you need two differences to locate a point, so three transmitters; in 3-D you need three differences, so four satellites. Either way, giving up on knowing the clock costs exactly one extra measurement. The next section gets to the same count by a route that's easier to compute.

    6The fix: make the clock error an unknown

    Here is the idea that makes GPS work, and it's almost cheeky. We don't know $b$. We don't know $x$ or $y$ either. So stop treating $b$ as an annoying error and treat it as one more unknown to solve for, exactly like the coordinates. Every measurement is an equation:

    $$\rho_i = \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2} + c\,b, \qquad i = 1, 2, \dots$$

    In the 2-D toy world there are now three unknowns, $(x, y, b)$, so we need three transmitters. In the real 3-D world the unknowns are $(x, y, z, b)$, four of them, and that is why a GPS receiver needs signals from at least four satellites. The fourth satellite is not there to make the position more precise; it is there to work out what time it is.

    Key idea

    A receiver doesn't need an accurate clock. It needs one more satellite. Solving for the clock error turns a cheap phone into a timing instrument as good as the satellites' atomic clocks, for as long as it keeps solving. As a side effect, GPS is one of the most widely used ways of distributing precise time, to phone networks and power grids among others.

    Solving it: guess, linearise, correct, repeat

    We still have a system of nonlinear equations (those square roots again). Receivers solve it the way you'd find your way in fog: start with a guess, see how wrong it is, take a step that should fix it, and repeat. The trick is that near the guess, the equations are almost linear, and linear equations are easy.

    Take a guess $\hat p$ for the position and $\hat b$ for the clock error. From the guess we can predict what each pseudorange should be, $\hat\rho_i = \lvert \hat p - s_i\rvert + c\hat b$, and compare it with what we measured. The mismatch $r_i = \rho_i - \hat\rho_i$ is called the residual. Now ask: how would the predicted range change if we nudged the guess?

    $$\lvert \hat p + \Delta p - s_i\rvert \approx \lvert \hat p - s_i\rvert + u_i \cdot \Delta p$$
    moving by a small step $\Delta p$ changes your distance from the satellite only by the part of the step that points along the line of sight; $u_i$ is the unit vector from $s_i$ towards $\hat p$, and $u_i \cdot \Delta p$ is that part (a sideways step barely changes the distance)
    $$\rho_i \approx \lvert \hat p - s_i\rvert + u_i \cdot \Delta p + c\,(\hat b + \Delta b)$$
    substitute into the pseudorange equation, with the unknown clock error written as guess plus correction
    $$r_i = \rho_i - \hat\rho_i \approx u_i \cdot \Delta p + c\,\Delta b$$
    subtract the predicted range $\hat\rho_i$: what's left is linear in the corrections $\Delta p$ and $\Delta b$
    $$\begin{pmatrix} r_1 \\ r_2 \\ r_3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{1x} & u_{1y} & c \\ u_{2x} & u_{2y} & c \\ u_{3x} & u_{3y} & c \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta x \\ \Delta y \\ \Delta b \end{pmatrix}$$
    one row per transmitter: a system of three linear equations in three unknowns, solvable by ordinary elimination
    $$\hat p \leftarrow \hat p + \Delta p, \qquad \hat b \leftarrow \hat b + \Delta b$$
    apply the corrections and repeat from the top; each pass the linear approximation is better, because the guess is closer

    Notice the matrix's last column: every entry is the same $c$. That column is the clock error at work, adding the same amount to every range, exactly as in the pseudorange definition. The first columns are the directions to the transmitters, so the matrix is a portrait of the geometry. Section 7 shows that this geometry decides how well the system can be solved.

    This procedure is called Gauss–Newton iteration (with more measurements than unknowns, each step is a least-squares fit, which a real receiver uses to average over all the satellites it can hear). It is the same idea as Newton's method for finding roots: replace a curve by its tangent line, jump to where the tangent says the answer is, repeat.

    Worked example · three transmitters, a 10-microsecond clock error

    Transmitters at A = (8, 34), B = (54, 36), C = (30, 4), in km. The phone is really at (34, 22), and its clock is 10 µs fast, so every pseudorange is $c \times 10\ \mu\text{s} = 2.998$ km too long. The true distances are 28.636, 24.413 and 18.439 km; the measured pseudoranges are 31.634, 27.411 and 21.437 km. The receiver knows only those, plus the transmitter positions.

    Start by guessing the centre of the three transmitters, (30.67, 24.67), with $b = 0$. Then iterate:

    stepguess $x$ (km)guess $y$ (km)guess $b$ (µs)position error
    030.66724.6670.0004.27 km
    133.84222.11110.711193 m
    233.999622.000410.00190.57 m
    334.000022.000010.00000.004 mm

    Three steps from a guess kilometres off to the exact answer, clock error included. Look at how the error shrinks: 4 km, 0.2 km, 0.0006 km. Each error is roughly proportional to the square of the one before, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles per step. That's the signature of Newton-style methods, and it's because the linear approximation's mistake shrinks with the square of the distance from the answer. A real receiver also has a great head start: once it has a fix, the previous one is an excellent first guess.

    Careful · exactly enough equations can have two answers

    With exactly as many measurements as unknowns, these nonlinear equations can occasionally have two valid solutions, just as two circles cross twice. Which one the iteration finds depends on the starting guess. In practice receivers usually track more satellites than the minimum, and the extra equations rule the impostor out.

    7Geometry matters: dilution of precision

    So far the measurements were exact. Real pseudoranges carry small random errors: noise in the receiver, leftover atmospheric delays (section 9), residual satellite clock and orbit errors. If each range is off by a metre or so, is the position also off by a metre or so?

    It depends on where the satellites are, and the trilateration widget already hinted why. When two circles cross at a wide angle, nudging one circle moves the crossing a little. When they cross at a shallow angle, nearly tangent, the same nudge slides the crossing a long way along the circles. Satellites spread all around the sky give wide crossing angles; satellites bunched in one direction give shallow ones.

    The clock unknown makes clustering even worse. If every satellite is in roughly the same direction, then moving towards them shortens every range by about the same amount, and that looks almost exactly like a change in the clock error, which also changes every range by the same amount. The receiver can't tell “I moved” from “my clock is off”, so small noise produces big swings in both.

    Definition · dilution of precision (DOP)

    If each range measurement has random error with standard deviation $\sigma$ (independent, equal for all satellites), the position error has a spread of about $\text{DOP} \times \sigma$. The DOP is a pure number computed only from the directions to the satellites, which are the rows $(u_{ix}, u_{iy}, 1)$ of the same matrix as in section 6, with the clock column measured in distance units. Small DOP (near 1 to 2) means good geometry; a large DOP means range errors get magnified. For a 2-D position it's called horizontal DOP, or HDOP.

    DOP is computed from the matrix $H$ whose rows are those direction vectors: $\text{HDOP} = \sqrt{Q_{xx} + Q_{yy}}$, where $Q = (H^{\mathsf T} H)^{-1}$. You don't need the formula to use the widget; it only says “how much does the least-squares solution amplify noise for this arrangement”. The widget checks it the honest way, by simulation.

    Notice the shape of the cloud when the transmitters are clustered: it stretches along the direction towards them. That's the “moved, or clock off?” confusion made visible. There's a real-world version of this you may never have noticed: all GPS satellites you can see are above you; none is below the horizon. So vertical position is always somewhat confused with clock error, and GPS altitude is typically noticeably less accurate than horizontal position.

    8Relativity: clocks in orbit tick at a different rate

    We've demanded nanosecond timing from the satellite clocks. Here's a problem that has nothing to do with engineering: a clock in orbit genuinely does not tick at the same rate as a clock on the ground, and two separate effects from Einstein's theories are responsible.

    Speed slows the satellite clock (special relativity). A clock moving relative to you ticks slower, by a fraction of about $v^2 / 2c^2$ when $v$ is much smaller than $c$. At about 3.9 km/s, that's a fraction of about $8 \times 10^{-11}$, which adds up to roughly 7 microseconds lost per day.

    Weaker gravity speeds it up (general relativity). Clocks deeper in a gravitational field tick slower. The satellites are far up, where Earth's gravity is weaker than at the surface, so their clocks tick faster than ground clocks, by roughly 45 microseconds per day.

    The gravity effect wins. Net, a GPS satellite clock runs fast by about 38 microseconds per day compared with a clock on the ground. Through our conversion rate, 38 µs × $c$ ≈ 11 km. That is the figure usually quoted: the error the drift represents, growing by about 11 km every day if nothing were done.

    $$\frac{\Delta f}{f}\Big|_{\text{gravity}} \approx \frac{GM}{c^2}\left(\frac{1}{R_E} - \frac{1}{r}\right)$$
    gravitational effect: proportional to the difference in gravitational potential $-GM/r$ between the surface (radius $R_E \approx 6{,}371$ km) and the orbit (radius $r$); $GM \approx 3.986 \times 10^{14}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}^2$ for Earth
    $$\frac{\Delta f}{f}\Big|_{\text{speed}} \approx -\frac{v^2}{2c^2} = -\frac{GM}{2 r c^2}$$
    special-relativistic slowing; for a circular orbit gravity supplies the centripetal force, $v^2/r = GM/r^2$, so $v^2 = GM/r$
    $$r = 26{,}571\ \text{km}: \quad +5.29 \times 10^{-10} \;-\; 0.83 \times 10^{-10} = +4.46 \times 10^{-10}$$
    plug in GPS: $r = 6{,}371 + 20{,}200$ km
    $$4.46 \times 10^{-10} \times 86{,}400\ \text{s} \approx 38.5\ \mu\text{s per day}$$
    multiply the fractional rate by the seconds in a day: about +45.7 from gravity and −7.2 from speed

    The fix is beautifully simple, and it was built in from the start: before launch, each satellite's clock is set to tick slightly slow, by almost exactly this fraction (its nominal 10.23 MHz reference is offset to about 10.229 999 995 43 MHz), so that once in orbit it ticks at the ground rate. Because the real orbits are slightly elliptical, the satellite's altitude and speed vary a little around each orbit, and receivers apply a further small correction for that, computed from the broadcast orbit.

    Careful · what the “11 km per day” really means

    It's the range-equivalent of the clock drift, and the standard way to show the size of the effect. But think back to section 6: an error that is the same on every satellite looks just like a receiver clock error, and the receiver would solve it away into $b$. So positions wouldn't literally walk off by 11 km a day. What would break is subtler and still fatal: GPS time would race away from time on the ground, the orbit predictions (which are tied to ground time) would go wrong, and the parts of the effect that differ between satellites wouldn't cancel. Relativity is not an optional correction for GPS.

    9What still goes wrong, and how phones cope

    With good clocks, relativity handled, and good geometry, why isn't GPS perfect? Because the formula $d = c\,\Delta t$ also assumes the signal travels in a straight line, at $c$, straight to you. Near the Earth, none of those is quite true.

    The ionosphere slows the signal. Between about 50 and 1,000 km up, the atmosphere contains a layer of charged particles that delays radio signals, by an amount that varies with the time of day, the season and solar activity. Uncorrected, it can add several metres to tens of metres to a range. Single-frequency receivers use a broadcast model that removes part of it. Better receivers listen on two frequencies: the ionosphere delays different frequencies by different amounts (the delay scales as $1/f^2$), so comparing them measures the delay directly and removes most of it. Many recent phones receive two frequency bands for exactly this reason.

    The lower atmosphere (troposphere) adds a smaller delay, about 2.4 m for a satellite straight overhead and more for satellites low on the horizon, whose signals cross more air. It's predictable enough to model well.

    Multipath: the signal bounces. Next to buildings, the signal can reach the antenna both directly and after reflecting off a wall. A reflection always travels further, so it makes the range look too long. When the direct path is blocked entirely, the receiver may lock onto a reflection only, and that range can be wrong by tens of metres.

    Urban canyons combine everything bad. Tall buildings block most of the sky, so the visible satellites are few and bunched overhead in a narrow strip: poor geometry, and a high DOP, from section 7. Many of the signals that do arrive are reflections. That's why your blue dot wanders across the street downtown, while under an open sky a smartphone's GPS is typically accurate to within about 5 metres (the figure the US government's gps.gov gives is 4.9 m).

    Assisted GPS (A-GPS). There's also a speed problem. The navigation message, which carries the orbit data, is sent at just 50 bits per second, so a receiver starting from nothing needs at least about 30 seconds of clean signal per satellite to collect it, and much longer to collect the full almanac. A phone shortcuts this: it downloads the orbit data over the mobile network or Wi-Fi, along with a rough position (from cell towers) and a rough time. Knowing roughly where it is and which satellites should be overhead, it only has to search a narrow range of codes and timings, so a first fix takes seconds instead of a minute or more. Phones also blend in other clues: Wi-Fi and cell positioning, motion sensors, and other satellite systems (Europe's Galileo, Russia's GLONASS, China's BeiDou), which use the same principles you've just learned. More satellites mean more equations and, usually, better geometry.

    The whole idea in one breath

    Each satellite says where it is and when it spoke; the delay tells you a distance, but measured on a bad clock, so every distance is off by the same unknown amount. Treat that amount as one more unknown, use one more satellite, and solve the equations by repeatedly linearising. How well that works depends on the geometry, and it only works at all because relativity was built into the satellites' clocks.

    1. Compute. A receiver's measurement of one signal's arrival time is off by 50 nanoseconds. Roughly how big is the resulting range error? And if the same 50 ns error affects every satellite equally, how big is the position error, assuming the receiver solves for its clock?

    Show answer

    $50 \times 10^{-9}\ \text{s} \times 3 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s} \approx 15$ m of range error. But if the same error is on every satellite, it's indistinguishable from a receiver clock error: it simply gets absorbed into the solved value of $b$, and the position is unaffected. Only errors that differ between satellites damage the position. This is why the common-error idea is so powerful.

    2. Count. A ship knows it is on the sea surface (so, treating the local sea as a flat plane, its height is known), but its receiver has a quartz clock. How many satellites does it need for a fix? Explain using unknowns and equations.

    Show answer

    Unknowns: two horizontal coordinates and the clock error, three in all (the height is known). Each satellite gives one pseudorange equation, so three satellites suffice. That's the 2-D toy world of this lesson. Real receivers do use a known altitude like this as a constraint when fewer than four satellites are visible.

    3. Predict. Four satellites are visible, all within a few degrees of each other low in the eastern sky. The receiver reports a position. Before computing anything, which direction do you expect its errors to be largest in, and why?

    Show answer

    Along the east–west direction (towards and away from the satellites). Moving towards them changes all four ranges by almost the same amount, which looks almost exactly like a change in clock error, so the receiver can barely separate “moved east” from “clock is off”. The DOP is large and the error cloud is a long streak pointing at the satellites, as in the clustered setting of the widget.

    4. Explain the bug. Someone argues: “Since the satellite clocks run 38 µs/day fast, GPS positions must drift 11 km per day unless the receiver corrects each day.” What's wrong with this argument, and what is right about it?

    Show answer

    Two things are wrong. First, the correction isn't done daily by receivers: the satellite clocks are built to tick slow by the right fraction before launch, so the drift never happens in the first place. Second, a drift common to all satellites would largely be absorbed into the receiver's clock solution rather than appear as position error. What's right: the effect is real and large (38 µs × $c$ ≈ 11 km), and without the correction GPS time and the orbit data would become inconsistent with ground time, which would break the system.

    Where to go next. Everything here used code timing, good to a few metres. Survey-grade receivers reach centimetres by also measuring the phase of the radio carrier wave itself (wavelength about 19 cm) and by comparing with a nearby base station whose position is known, so that shared errors cancel in differences, the same trick as in section 5. That's a lesson of its own: differential GPS and carrier-phase positioning.
