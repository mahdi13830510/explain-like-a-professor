    The Derivative

    Average speed is easy: distance divided by time. But what does it mean to have a speed at one instant, when no time passes and no distance is covered? Answering that question is the derivative.

    This part: average rate of change → shrinking the interval → limits → the derivative → why zooming in makes curves straight → the derivative of x² seen as a growing square → the power rule → where it breaks, including on a real computer.
    Next parts: the chain rule (Part 2), and derivatives in many dimensions: gradients (Part 3).
    Assumed: algebra and graphs of functions. Nothing else.

    1The problem: speed at an instant

    Drop a stone from a bridge. Ignoring air resistance, physics says the distance it has fallen after $t$ seconds is

    $$s(t) = 4.9\,t^2 \quad \text{metres},$$

    where the 4.9 is half of Earth's gravitational acceleration, $g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. After 1 second it has fallen 4.9 m, after 2 seconds 19.6 m, after 3 seconds 44.1 m. Notice that the gaps grow: the stone covers more ground each second than the second before, because it's speeding up.

    So here's the question: how fast is the stone moving at exactly $t = 2$ seconds?

    The only definition of speed we have is distance ÷ time. Try to apply it at a single instant, and both the distance and the time are zero: we get $0/0$, which is meaningless. A speedometer still shows a number at every instant, though, so there must be a sensible meaning. Making that meaning precise is the whole job of this lesson, and the tool we build, the derivative, measures how fast anything changes: temperature, a loss function, a population, a price.

    2What we can compute: average rate of change

    Start with what does make sense. Between two times $t_1$ and $t_2$, the stone falls $s(t_2) - s(t_1)$ metres in $t_2 - t_1$ seconds, so its average speed over that interval is

    $$\text{average speed} = \frac{s(t_2) - s(t_1)}{t_2 - t_1} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}.$$

    The symbol $\Delta$ ("delta") just means "change in": $\Delta t$ is the elapsed time, $\Delta s$ the distance covered.

    On a graph of distance against time, this number has a picture. Mark the two points $(t_1, s(t_1))$ and $(t_2, s(t_2))$ on the curve and join them with a straight line. That line rises $\Delta s$ while it runs $\Delta t$, so its slope (rise over run) is the average speed. A line through two points of a curve is called a secant line.

    Definition · average rate of change

    For a function $f$ and two inputs $a$ and $a+h$ with $h \neq 0$, the average rate of change of $f$ over that interval is $$\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}.$$ Here $a$ is where the interval starts, $h$ is its length (the run), and the numerator is the change in output (the rise). This expression is called the difference quotient, and it is the slope of the secant line through $(a, f(a))$ and $(a+h, f(a+h))$.

    We've written the interval as "from $a$ to $a + h$" rather than "from $t_1$ to $t_2$" because it lets us talk about the interval's length $h$ directly. In a moment, $h$ is what we'll shrink.

    3Shrinking the interval

    Average speed over a long interval blurs together moments when the stone was slow and moments when it was fast. To get closer to the speed at $t = 2$, average over shorter and shorter intervals starting there. Let's compute. With $a = 2$:

    interval length $h$ (s)$s(2+h) - s(2)$ (m)average speed (m/s)
    124.524.5
    0.511.02522.05
    0.12.00920.09

    The averages are settling down. Each time $h$ shrinks tenfold, the excess over 19.6 also shrinks tenfold: 0.49, then 0.049, then 0.0049. Everything points at 19.6 m/s. Watch it happen geometrically:

    Why exactly 19.6? Algebra makes the pattern certain

    A table can suggest a value; it can't prove one. So compute the difference quotient for a general $h$, keeping $h$ as a symbol. Click through one step at a time:

    $$\frac{s(2+h) - s(2)}{h} = \frac{4.9(2+h)^2 - 4.9\cdot 2^2}{h}$$
    substitute $s(t) = 4.9t^2$ at $t = 2+h$ and $t = 2$
    $$= \frac{4.9\,(4 + 4h + h^2) - 4.9\cdot 4}{h}$$
    expand $(2+h)^2 = 4 + 2\cdot 2\cdot h + h^2$
    $$= \frac{4.9\,(4h + h^2)}{h}$$
    the $4.9 \cdot 4$ terms cancel: the starting distance drops out
    $$= 4.9\,(4 + h)$$
    divide top and bottom by $h$, which is allowed because $h \neq 0$
    $$= 19.6 + 4.9\,h$$
    distribute: this is exactly the pattern in the table (excess $= 4.9h$)

    Now the mystery dissolves. The average speed over an interval of length $h$ is exactly $19.6 + 4.9h$. The extra $4.9h$ is the bias caused by the stone speeding up during the interval, and it disappears as the interval does. There is precisely one number that $19.6 + 4.9h$ approaches as $h$ approaches 0: 19.6.

    Careful · the step everyone gets wrong

    We never set $h = 0$ in the original fraction; that gives $0/0$. We simplified while $h$ was still nonzero, which made the troublesome $h$ in the denominator cancel. Only then did we ask what happens as $h$ shrinks. That order is the whole trick, and it's why the next idea, the limit, is phrased the way it is.

    4Limits: approaching without arriving

    We need language for "the value that $19.6 + 4.9h$ heads toward as $h$ gets close to 0, without $h$ ever being 0." That is a limit.

    Definition · limit (working version)

    We write $\displaystyle\lim_{h \to 0} g(h) = L$ if $g(h)$ can be made as close to $L$ as we like by taking $h$ close enough to 0, but not equal to 0.

    Precisely: for every tolerance $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a distance $\delta > 0$ such that whenever $0 < |h| < \delta$, we have $|g(h) - L| < \varepsilon$.

    Read the precise version as a challenge game. You name any tolerance $\varepsilon$, however tiny ("get within 0.0001 of 19.6"). I must answer with a $\delta$ such that every $h$ within $\delta$ of zero (except zero itself) lands within your tolerance. For our stone, $|g(h) - 19.6| = 4.9|h|$, so I answer $\delta = \varepsilon / 4.9$, and I win every round. That's what it means for the limit to be 19.6. The phrase "$0 < |h|$" is the formal version of "never at zero".

    5The derivative

    Put the pieces together. The speed at an instant is the limit of average speeds over shrinking intervals. And nothing about this used the fact that $s$ was a distance, so the same construction measures the instantaneous rate of change of any function.

    Definition · derivative

    The derivative of $f$ at the input $a$ is $$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h},$$ provided this limit exists. In words: the instantaneous rate of change of $f$ at $a$; geometrically, the slope of the tangent line to the graph at $(a, f(a))$. Its units are output units per input unit (here, metres per second).

    So the stone's speed at $t = 2$ is $s'(2) = 19.6$ m/s, about 70.6 km/h. If the limit doesn't exist, we say $f$ is not differentiable at $a$, and §9 shows how that happens.

    There's a second notation you'll see everywhere, due to Leibniz: $\dfrac{df}{dx}$. It's a reminder of where the number came from, a ratio of a small change in $f$ to a small change in $x$, taken in the limit. It is not literally a fraction of two numbers, but it behaves enough like one that the notation pays for itself in Part 2.

    6The picture: zoom in until the curve is straight

    Here's the geometric heart of the derivative, and the intuition worth keeping for life. Take a smooth curve and zoom in on one point. The more you zoom, the less the curve bends within your view, until it is indistinguishable from a straight line. The slope of that line is the derivative. A differentiable function is one that looks linear up close.

    This explains why derivatives are so useful. Near any point, a complicated differentiable function can be replaced by a simple line, $f(a + h) \approx f(a) + f'(a)\,h$, and the error is small compared with $h$. Much of science and machine learning runs on that one approximation. Gradient descent, for example, is nothing but "follow the local straight line downhill".

    7The derivative of $x^2$, seen as a growing square

    So far we've computed one number, $s'(2)$. Can we get the derivative at every point at once, as a function? Take the simplest interesting case, $f(x) = x^2$, and think of $x^2$ literally: it's the area of a square with side $x$.

    Grow the side by a little $h$. The new area is $(x+h)^2$. How much area did we add? Look at the picture before doing algebra:

    The picture is the derivation. Here it is in symbols, each step matching a piece of the square:

    $$\frac{(x+h)^2 - x^2}{h}$$
    added area divided by the growth, the difference quotient for $f(x) = x^2$
    $$= \frac{x^2 + 2xh + h^2 - x^2}{h}$$
    expand: old square + two strips $xh$ + corner $h^2$
    $$= \frac{2xh + h^2}{h}$$
    the old square cancels: only the added area remains
    $$= 2x + h$$
    divide by $h \neq 0$: strips give $2x$, the corner gives $h$
    $$\xrightarrow{\;h \to 0\;} 2x$$
    the corner's contribution disappears in the limit
    Key idea

    $\dfrac{d}{dx}\,x^2 = 2x$. The derivative is a function: feed it any point, and it returns the slope there. The two strips are the "first-order" change, proportional to $h$; the corner is "second-order", proportional to $h^2$. Derivatives keep the first-order part and throw away everything smaller.

    Check it against the stone: $s(t) = 4.9t^2$, so $s'(t) = 4.9 \cdot 2t = 9.8t$, and $s'(2) = 19.6$, matching what we found the long way. (Multiplying a function by a constant multiplies its derivative by that constant. Pull 4.9 through the limit to see why.)

    8The power rule

    The square picture generalises. For $f(x) = x^3$, think of a cube of side $x$ growing by $h$: the added volume is three thin slabs $x^2 h$, plus thinner rods and a tiny corner cube. The slabs dominate, so the derivative should be $3x^2$. For general $x^n$ we need the expansion of $(x+h)^n$, and we only need its first two terms:

    $$(x+h)^n = \underbrace{(x+h)(x+h)\cdots(x+h)}_{n \text{ factors}}$$
    definition of the power
    $$= x^n + n\,x^{n-1}h + (\text{terms containing } h^2, h^3, \ldots)$$
    expanding, each term picks $x$ or $h$ from every factor. Picking $h$ from no factor gives $x^n$; picking $h$ from exactly one of the $n$ factors gives $n$ copies of $x^{n-1}h$; everything else has at least two $h$'s
    $$\frac{(x+h)^n - x^n}{h} = n\,x^{n-1} + (\text{terms containing } h, h^2, \ldots)$$
    subtract $x^n$, divide by $h \neq 0$: each leftover term keeps at least one factor of $h$
    $$\frac{d}{dx}\,x^n = n\,x^{n-1}$$
    take $h \to 0$: every term with an $h$ vanishes

    This is the power rule. Notice that it's the growing-square argument in disguise: "$n$ copies of $x^{n-1}h$" are the $n$ slabs, and the higher-order terms are the corners. The derivation as written holds for whole numbers $n \ge 1$. The rule turns out to be true for every real exponent ($\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x} = \frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$, for example), but proving that needs tools from Part 2.

    Worked example · the falling stone, all at once

    $s(t) = 4.9t^2$ gives $s'(t) = 4.9 \cdot 2t = 9.8t$ by the power rule and the constant multiple. So the speed is $9.8$ m/s after 1 s, $19.6$ m/s after 2 s, and $29.4$ m/s after 3 s. The speed grows by exactly 9.8 m/s every second. That constant rate of change of speed is the acceleration $g$, and it says the derivative of the speed is $g$: a derivative of a derivative, which we'll meet again.

    Interpretation and limits: real stones feel air resistance, which grows with speed, so real speeds fall below $9.8t$ and eventually level off at a terminal velocity. The derivative is exact; the model $s = 4.9t^2$ is the approximation.

    9Where the derivative breaks

    The definition asks for a limit to exist, and sometimes it doesn't. Three ways to fail are worth recognising:

    Corners. For $f(x) = |x|$ at 0, the difference quotient is $|h|/h$, which equals $+1$ for $h > 0$ and $-1$ for $h < 0$. Approaching from the right and from the left gives different answers, so there is no single limit and no derivative. You saw this in the zoom widget: the corner never straightens. This isn't exotic. The ReLU function used in nearly every neural network, $\max(0, x)$, has exactly this corner at 0, and deep-learning libraries simply choose a value there.

    Jumps. If the function jumps, say a price that steps up at midnight, the difference quotient blows up as $h \to 0$.

    Vertical tangents. $f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}$ is smooth-looking at 0, but its tangent there is vertical, with "infinite slope", so the limit doesn't exist as a number.

    On a real computer: why you can't just use a tiny h

    If the derivative is the limit as $h \to 0$, why not just compute $\frac{f(a+h) - f(a)}{h}$ with a very small $h$, like $10^{-15}$? Try it. The figure below computes the difference quotient of $\sin$ at $a = 1$ live in your browser, in standard double-precision arithmetic, and compares it with the true derivative, $\cos 1$.

    Two errors fight here. The truncation error, from using a finite $h$, is about $\frac{h}{2}|f''(a)|$ and shrinks with $h$. The rounding error, from subtracting nearly equal numbers, is about $\frac{\epsilon |f(a)|}{h}$, where $\epsilon \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-16}$ is double-precision's relative precision, and it grows as $h$ shrinks. Their sum is smallest near $h \approx \sqrt{\epsilon} \approx 10^{-8}$, giving about 8 correct digits, which is what you see in the figure. This is why machine learning doesn't differentiate networks numerically: it uses exact symbolic rules, the subject of Part 2, organised as backpropagation.

    ✓Check your understanding

    1. Predict. A function $f$ has $f(3) = 7$ and $f'(3) = 2$. Without knowing anything else, estimate $f(3.1)$. When would your estimate be poor?

    Show answer

    Local linearity gives $f(3.1) \approx f(3) + f'(3)\cdot 0.1 = 7 + 0.2 = 7.2$. The estimate is poor when $f$ curves sharply near 3, that is, when the "corner" (second-order) term is large compared with $f'(3)\,h$, or when 0.1 isn't small at the scale on which $f$ bends.

    2. Derive. Using only the definition (no power rule), show that the derivative of $f(x) = 3x + 5$ is 3 everywhere. Then explain in one sentence why the answer had to be a constant, using the zoom picture.

    Show answer

    $\frac{3(x+h)+5-(3x+5)}{h} = \frac{3h}{h} = 3$ for every $h \neq 0$, so the limit is 3. A line is already straight; zooming in changes nothing, so the slope is the same everywhere.

    3. Explain the bug. A student computes the derivative of $x^2$ at $x = 5$ by writing $\frac{(5+h)^2 - 25}{h}$ and "plugging in $h = 0$" to get $0/0$, and concludes the derivative doesn't exist. What went wrong, and what is the right answer?

    Show answer

    They substituted before simplifying. While $h \neq 0$, the quotient equals $\frac{10h + h^2}{h} = 10 + h$, whose limit as $h \to 0$ is 10. The limit never evaluates the quotient at $h = 0$; it asks what the values approach.
